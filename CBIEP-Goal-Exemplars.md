# CBIEP Goal Exemplars

## About This Knowledge Source

- This document contains curated, gold-standard CBIEP (Competency-Based Individual Education Plan) goal exemplars.
- The exemplars model the expected level of specificity, tone, structure, and measurement for CBIEP goals.
- The exemplars are not exhaustive.
- Each exemplar is a model to be adapted to student-specific anecdotal information. Exemplars are not to be copied verbatim into a student's plan.
- "XYZ" and "XZY" are placeholders for the student's name.

## CBIEP Goal Structure

Every CBIEP goal contains five components, in this order:

| Component | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Goal** | A first-person "I can…" or "I…" statement drawn from a core competency. |
| **Rationale** | A strengths-first narrative describing the student, the barrier to learning, the conditions that make it harder or easier, and why the skill matters for school and life beyond school. |
| **Objective** | A specific, first-person "I can…" statement describing the observable skill the student will practise. |
| **Strategies** | Two lists: what **supportive adults** (teachers, EAs) will do, and what **the student** will do. |
| **Measurement** | A "We will know that… when…" statement, the sources of evidence, and where triangulated evidence is recorded. |

## Common Patterns Across the Exemplars

- The Rationale opens with the student's strengths (e.g., thoughtful, curious, diligent, good humour, attends regularly).
- The Rationale names the barrier in terms of conditions and environment (e.g., unclear expectations, high cognitive load, fast pace, social exposure), not as a deficit of the student.
- The Rationale draws on student self-report ("XYZ reports that…") and, where relevant, family, medical, or teacher input.
- The Rationale ends by connecting the skill to post-secondary, employment, or life beyond high school.
- Adult strategies are concrete and observable, and often include example prompts in quotation marks.
- Adult strategies state what adults will avoid or not expect (e.g., vague prompts, forced eye contact, group projects at this time).
- Student strategies are small, achievable actions, often "with teacher support" or "when prompted."
- Measurement relies on triangulated evidence: teacher observation, student feedback/self-report, and feedback from other supporting adults (EAs, Inclusive Ed teachers).
- Measurement names the database where evidence is recorded. Names used in the exemplars: **GISS CBIEP Feedback database**, **GISS CBIEP Progress Feedback database**, **GISS CBIEP Progress database**, **GISS CBIEP Evidence Tracker**.

## Exemplar 1: Communication – Understanding Task Expectations

**Focus area:** Communication; task clarity; teacher-initiated check-ins
**Keywords:** unclear expectations, confusion, concrete prompts, paraphrasing tasks, self-advocacy, dignity

### Goal
I can communicate to understand learning expectations and navigate classroom tasks.

### Rationale
XYZ is thoughtful and willing to engage. However, high workload and unclear expectations can cause confusion before XYZ is able to identify or articulate what support is needed. Open-ended check-ins can increase uncertainty rather than provide clarity. Learning access improves when teachers initiate communication using concrete, task-specific prompts that reduce ambiguity, and check in with him frequently to make sure XYZ is able to paraphrase the task at hand and is equipped to complete it. Developing this responsive communication pattern supports dignity, engagement, and gradual independence, while preparing XYZ to navigate increasingly complex learning systems beyond high school.

### Objective
I can show my understanding of a task when a teacher asks me clear, concrete questions about what to do next.

### Strategies

**Supportive adults will:**
- Use specific, task-focused questions (e.g., "What is the first step?", "What do you do after opening the document?").
- Avoid vague or abstract prompts such as "How are you doing?" or "Do you get it?".
- Break directions into single-step check-ins to confirm understanding.
- Provide clarification before confusion escalates, rather than waiting for the student to self-advocate.
- Pair verbal prompts with visual or written cues when possible.

**The student will:**
- Respond to concrete teacher questions by describing the next step or showing what to do.
- Use teacher prompts to re-orient to the task when unsure.
- Engage in the task after clarification without needing to independently generate questions.

### Measurement
We will know that XYZ has met this objective when the student reports increased understanding of tasks following teacher prompts. Progress will be measured through teacher observation, student feedback, and feedback from supporting adults. Triangulated evidence regarding progress towards this objective will be recorded in the GISS CBIEP Feedback database.

## Exemplar 2: Executive Function – Organization (Binder and Calendar)

**Focus area:** Executive function; organization; task initiation and follow-through
**Keywords:** binder system, iOS calendar, cognitive load, overwhelm, anxiety, materials, deadlines

### Goal
I can plan, organize, and keep track of my learning tasks using effective strategies.

### Rationale
XYZ is attending regularly and wants to do well in class. He brings curiosity, willingness, and good humour to learning. XYZ reports that he has challenges with task initiation and follow-through in complex classroom environments. Especially when materials and expectations are not clearly organized, cognitive load increases and learning momentum is disrupted; the student reports that he often does not know what to do. This can lead to overwhelm and anxiety. Difficulty tracking materials and deadlines can result in uncertainty about what to do next, even when XYZ is motivated to participate. He would benefit from strengthening organizational systems to increase clarity, support engagement, reduce overwhelm, and increase confidence across classes. These skills are essential for navigating the increased independence and complexity of senior secondary learning and for future post-secondary or life contexts.

### Objective
I can organize my learning materials and keep track of my tasks by using a structured binder system and calendar reminders.

### Strategies

**Supportive adults will:**
- Explicitly teach and model a consistent binder structure (one clearly labelled section per class; no loose papers).
- Support the student in identifying where new handouts belong at the time they are given, rather than expecting later sorting.
- Prompt the student to enter tasks into the iOS calendar during class using simple, concrete language (e.g., "Open your calendar and add this assignment for tomorrow").
- Ensure assignment reminders include brief, clear titles rather than long descriptions to reduce cognitive load.
- Use written and verbal reminders together when referencing upcoming tasks.

**The student will:**
- Place materials into the appropriate binder section when prompted.
- Enter assignments or work reminders into the iOS calendar with teacher support.
- Refer to the binder and calendar when asked what materials or tasks are needed for a class.

### Measurement
We will know that XYZ has met this objective when the student reports increased clarity about what materials and tasks are expected. Progress will be measured through teacher observation, student feedback, and review of binder organization and calendar entries. Triangulated evidence regarding progress towards this objective will be recorded in the GISS CBIEP Feedback database.

## Exemplar 3: Communication – Reciprocal Conversation and Active Listening

**Focus area:** Communication; social connection; active and whole-body listening
**Keywords:** eye contact, hoody, ear buds, sentence starters, processing time, reciprocal conversation, peers

### Goal
I listen and respond to others.

### Rationale
This student has progressed considerably with his task initiation and completion and continues to require some support to stay on track with this. His communication skills and connections with peers and supportive adults in the school setting are skills that he is developing. The student prefers not to engage in reciprocal communication with others and prefers to hide with his hoody on and eyes directed at the floor. In particular, he is working on active and whole-body listening and responding. He has been working on making eye contact when he communicates with others and has been taking risks and demonstrating this occasionally. Building his confidence with his communication, his ability to make eye contact, and removing his hoody when connecting with others are important skills to develop that will support this student's success outside of the school setting and throughout his life.

### Objective
I can engage in reciprocal conversations by responding to supportive adults and peers.

### Strategies

**Supportive adults will:**
- Model warm, respectful conversational behaviours (eye contact, calm tone, active listening).
- Initiate low-pressure check-ins to create predictable opportunities for reciprocal conversation.
- Use the student's name and approach gently to support felt safety.
- Give the student processing time before expecting a response.
- Use short, clear questions to make it easier for the student to respond.
- Positively reinforce any attempt at engagement (eye contact, brief verbal response, nodding, etc.).
- Support the student to scaffold his active listening and communication skills in small steps.
- Not expect the student to make eye contact throughout communication.
- Provide sentence starters or prompts the student can use in conversations.
- Avoid interrupting or rushing the student when he is trying to respond.
- Create opportunities for successful peer interactions in structured, supported settings.
- Scaffold and encourage him to remove his hoody when communicating.
- Provide gentle suggestions and invite the student to take risks (e.g., "Do you think you can try making some eye contact next time we chat?", "Do you think you can try removing your hoody while you are watching this student present?").

**The student will:**
- Work on removing his ear buds when listening to instruction and speaking with others.
- Work on keeping himself mentally present and actively listening.
- Take breaks when needed (usually reading or walking) to stay mentally present.
- Practise using sentence starters to keep conversations going if needed.
- Ask for repetition or clarification if unsure what someone said.
- Attempt one reciprocal conversation each day (with an adult or peer).

### Measurement
We will know the student has met this objective when he regularly responds when communicating with others. Progress will be measured through anecdotal evidence from teachers, EAs, and the student. Triangulated evidence regarding the progress of this objective will be recorded in the GISS CBIEP Progress Feedback database.

## Exemplar 4: Collaboration – Working With Peers

**Focus area:** Collaboration; peer interaction; employability skills
**Keywords:** partner work, group work refusal, solitary learner, employment, think-pair-share, turn and talk, small tasks

### Goal
In familiar situations, I connect, cooperate, and work with others for specific purposes.

### Rationale
This student has made progress with completing school work in the high school setting. His progress in doing independent learning activities has improved, and feedback from teachers is that he is resistant to and refuses any partner or group work. This student is solitary as he engages in his coursework. The student is motivated to get a job, and his family believes that his inability to connect with unfamiliar people is a major barrier to employment. In the work world outside of school, employers identify collaboration and an ability to work with others as one of the most important skills they look for in employees. To support this student's continued success in school and to facilitate success beyond school, working with others is an essential skill to develop that will serve him throughout his life.

### Objective
I can collaborate with others in short, purposeful interactions (such as sharing ideas, asking questions, or contributing to a group task).

### Strategies

**Supportive adults will:**
- Look for opportunities to request that the student complete a SMALL task with another student in the classroom setting (e.g., play a game; turn and talk; think, pair, share; one-question response).
- Assess any potential appropriate pairing or connection the student might have in the class and leverage that connection for a small cooperation or collaboration task.
- Celebrate ANY communication, cooperation, or collaboration with peers.
- "Notice" when the student does interact with peers, and subtly point out and "name" the interaction as an example of cooperation or collaboration. This will nurture future instances of cooperation and collaboration.
- Not expect the student to engage in partner or group projects at this time.

**The student will:**
- Take small steps towards cooperating and collaborating with peers.
- Find an opportunity to play a game with peers.
- Find an opportunity to talk to a peer and agree on an answer to a question from a teacher.

### Measurement
We will know that the student has met this objective when he has cooperated or collaborated with a peer on a task request or assignment from the teacher. Progress will be measured through teacher observation and student feedback. Triangulated evidence regarding progress towards this objective will be recorded in the GISS CBIEP Progress database.

## Exemplar 5: Executive Function – Prioritizing and Planning (Matrix and Pomodoro)

**Focus area:** Executive function; prioritization; perfectionism; help-seeking
**Keywords:** overthinking, paralysis, perfectionism, cognitive overload, reduced workload, prioritization matrix, Pomodoro method, support block, medical team recommendation

### Goal
I can set priorities; implement, monitor, and adjust a plan; and assess the results.

### Rationale
XYZ is a deep thinker and enjoys academic subjects of all kinds. She is interested in sciences, socials, philosophy, writing, reading, and maths. XYZ is also a very diligent person and has high expectations of her work. When XYZ gets assignments, she reports that she can overthink tasks and lose her way. When she gets stuck with her work, she might feel paralyzed and does not move forward, as she only wants to hand in complete and correct assignments. In previous school years, this has led to increased stress and falling behind. Her medical team has recommended that the student keep her workload at home to a minimum to avoid cognitive overload. XYZ has a support block each semester this school year and is interested in experimenting with tools and strategies that allow her to manage her work by prioritizing according to complexity and importance, while identifying which tasks she needs additional help with from the teacher. XYZ would benefit from clear instruction and timelines, as well as supports in the classroom to increase her ability to finish work during her support block. Organizing her work and building skills that allow her to reach her full academic potential while maintaining a regulated emotional state will be beneficial for XYZ's post-secondary endeavours and beyond.

### Objective
I can experiment with planning systems by using a matrix and the Pomodoro planning system to prioritize my work, and initiate communication with my teachers to clarify questions I might have about assignments.

### Strategies

**Teachers will:**
- Teach the Pomodoro planning system to the class.
- Introduce matrix systems that prioritize work to the class.
- Give clear instructions and timelines for assignments.
- Conduct one brief, task-specific check-in per block using a concrete prompt (e.g., "What is your next step?") to make sure she is on track and to encourage her to ask questions about her assignments.
- Flag immediately with the student and team if the student is at risk of falling behind.
- Allow the student sufficient time in conversations to explain her needs.
- Offer a reduced workload and alternative assignments to avoid cognitive overload.

**The student will:**
- Use the Pomodoro method to structure her study time during her support block.
- Use a prioritization matrix to identify which tasks she can do independently and which tasks need additional support from her teachers.
- Check in with her teachers and her Inclusive Ed Teacher regularly.

### Measurement
We will know that the student has reached this objective when they report that they feel successful in their learning by experimenting with tools and strategies to organize learning. Progress will be measured through anecdotal reports by the student and their teachers. Triangulated evidence regarding the progress towards this objective will be stored in the GISS CBIEP Evidence Tracker.

## Exemplar 6: Executive Function – Sequencing and Retention (Structured Guides)

**Focus area:** Executive function; information processing; memory and retention
**Keywords:** pace too fast, organizing information, sequential steps (first, next, then), explicit instruction, notes, structured guides, TASK program

### Goal
I can set priorities; implement, monitor, and adjust a plan; and assess the results.

### Rationale
XYZ wants to do well in school and has been accepted to the TASK program. XYZ is a very good tennis player and puts a lot of effort into everything he does. XYZ reports that he finds it challenging to organize the information he receives in his classes. He says that often things just go too fast and that he has difficulty keeping up. XYZ might understand a concept but has difficulty referring back to it in the following days. For his learning needs, structure is indispensable, and he would like to work on building skills that allow him to identify the important pieces of each lesson. XYZ would benefit from significant supports in breaking down tasks into sequential steps (first, next, then), and explicit instruction accompanied by notes that allow him to refer to previous lessons to make sense of current content. These skills will serve him beyond high school in all post-secondary plans he might have.

### Objective
I can refer to structured guides and sequential lists to understand and practise content that I learned in class.

### Strategies

**Teacher and EA will:**
- Use explicit instruction: clearly explain and demonstrate concepts, provide ample opportunities for practice, and offer feedback to ensure student success.
- Provide the student with sequential lists and processes that he can refer back to and practise with.
- Explicitly teach how to use sequential lists, notes, and practice content to improve retention of information.

**The student will:**
- Refer to guides, lists, and notes to practise content, jog his memory, and initiate work.

### Measurement
We will know XYZ is proficient with this objective when he is able to refer to guides and lists to access content and show his knowledge. This will be measured by observation, anecdotal notes, and interviews with the student. Triangulated evidence regarding progress towards this objective will be recorded in the GISS CBIEP Feedback database.

## Exemplar 7: Self-Advocacy – Social Anxiety and Safe Participation

**Focus area:** Self-advocacy; social-emotional regulation; participation
**Keywords:** social anxiety, fear of judgment, presentations, alternatives to whole-class presentations, advance notice, predictability, autism-related traits (undocumented), psychological safety

### Goal
I advocate for myself and my ideas; I accept myself.

### Rationale
The student experiences significant social anxiety, especially in situations where they may feel exposed, evaluated, or singled out by peers. This anxiety manifests as a heightened fear of being judged, making mistakes publicly, or being the focus of attention. These fears can activate a stress response that makes academic participation—particularly presentations, group work, or speaking tasks—overwhelming. Additionally, while a psychiatrist has noted characteristics consistent with autism spectrum behaviours, no formal documentation has been submitted. This information is still important because autism-related traits (e.g., cognitive rigidity, high sensitivity to unpredictability, social-communication stressors, difficulty perspective-shifting) can intensify the difficulty of moving out of fear-based patterns, even with reassurance. As a result, transitions, unexpected social demands, and evaluative situations may feel disproportionately threatening. Consistent, predictable, and compassionate supports from teachers can significantly reduce stress and create opportunities for the student to participate in ways that feel psychologically safe. Understanding and addressing these needs is crucial not only for her success in school but also for her long-term well-being and independence after graduation.

### Objective
I can take steps toward participating in class and collaborating with others at a pace that feels safe for me.

### Strategies

**Teachers will:**
- Provide alternatives to whole-class presentations (e.g., video recordings, small groups, or 1:1 presentations with the teacher).
- Offer advance notice for any activity requiring speaking, participating, or being called on.
- Check in privately to co-plan low-stress ways for the student to show their learning without social exposure.
- Create predictability by outlining what will happen in class, especially for activities involving peers.
- Validate the student's anxiety responses and scaffold participation in very small, manageable steps without pressure or forced exposure.

**The student will:**
- No student strategies are listed in the source exemplar for this goal.

### Measurement
We will know that XYZ has met this objective when she is able to participate in class to a degree that is manageable for her while maintaining social-emotional regulation. This will be measured by student and teacher reports. Triangulated evidence regarding the progress towards this objective will be stored in the GISS CBIEP Evidence Tracker.
